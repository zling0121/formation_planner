# FormationFlow Research Synthesis

## Executive Summary

**Recommendation: MODIFY.**

The available evidence supports further validation of a narrow formation-transition visualization concept, but it does not support building the full proposed MVP. The sole participant reported spending 5–8 hours designing 20–30 formations, encountering several rehearsal collisions and movement-path errors, and seeing value in visual transition simulation. However, the participant said formation design itself—not transition safety analysis—was the most time-consuming task. The participant also already used Google Slides and Arrange Us, and resolved problems through pre-rehearsal review, rehearsal communication, collaborative path clarification, and repeated practice. [I-01]

The evidence base contains one interview. Therefore, no behavior, pain point, preference, or product demand can be classified as repeated across users. The next step should be a testable prototype centered on two-formation visualization, followed by interviews and observed usability sessions with additional choreographers. Collision detection, travel measurement, automated swaps, locks, and recommendation controls should remain hypotheses until they receive direct evidence. [I-01]

## Severity Evidence

### Strongest severity signals

- **Time burden:** Formation design took approximately 5–8 hours across several days for a performance with roughly 20–30 formations. This is the only quantified burden in the source. [I-01]
- **Rehearsal disruption:** Several collisions and movement-path errors occurred during rehearsal, and some dancers did not exit or move through the formation correctly. [I-01]
- **Rework:** After revisions, the group practiced the updated transition several times to ensure understanding and retention. [I-01]
- **Cognitive burden:** The participant described value in seeing movement simulated instead of imagining every transition mentally. [I-01]

### Limits on severity claims

- The notes do not quantify how much of the 5–8 hours was spent on transitions versus formation creation. The participant explicitly identified formation design itself as the most time-consuming part. [I-01]
- The notes do not quantify rehearsal time lost, injury or near-injury, number of affected dancers, performance impact, emotional impact, or financial impact. [I-01]
- The collisions may partly reflect novice experience because this was the participant's first time designing formations. The evidence does not show whether experienced choreographers have the same severity. [I-01]
- No willingness to pay, urgency to adopt, abandonment of current tools, or failed deadline is reported. [I-01]

- **Pre-rehearsal review and simulation:** Used by the choreographer to reduce errors before rehearsal. The notes do not specify whether this occurred in a tool or mentally. [I-01]
- **In-rehearsal correction:** The choreographer and dancers worked together to identify clearer paths when collisions occurred. [I-01]
- **Repeated practice:** Updated transitions were rehearsed several times until dancers understood and remembered them. [I-01]
- **Sequential explanation:** Changes were communicated one formation at a time to clarify the complete transition. [I-01]
- **Occasional asynchronous reference:** Updated slides were sometimes shared for dancers to review outside rehearsal. [I-01]

These workarounds are not described as failures. Google Slides is explicitly credited with useful independent access, while rehearsal collaboration and repetition appear to resolve at least some transition problems. [I-01]

### Tensions within the single interview

These are not contradictions, but they matter for product direction:

- The participant valued visual transition simulation, yet identified designing the formations themselves as the most time-consuming step. This suggests transition evaluation may address a real problem without addressing the largest reported time burden. [I-01]
- The participant encountered collisions despite reviewing and simulating transitions beforehand. This suggests the current review method was helpful but incomplete; it does not establish that automated proximity detection is the required solution. [I-01]
- The participant used an existing dedicated formation-planning application while also relying on Google Slides. This may indicate unmet needs, complementary use cases, habit, accessibility, or sharing advantages, but the notes do not identify which explanation is correct. [I-01]
- Formation changes were primarily communicated during rehearsal even though updated slides could be shared outside rehearsal. This suggests asynchronous artifacts were useful but did not replace live instruction and practice. [I-01]

## Unsupported Assumptions

The following FormationFlow assumptions are not directly supported by the available interview:

- The primary market consistently consists of student choreographers managing groups of 6–16 dancers; I-01's student status and group size are not provided. [I-01]
- Transition problems recur across multiple choreographers, experience levels, dance styles, or group sizes. [I-01]
- Experienced choreographers experience collision and path errors at a level comparable to a first-time formation designer. [I-01]
- Straight-line, simultaneous movement accurately represents the transitions users need to evaluate. No path geometry or timing preference is described. [I-01]
- A numeric unsafe-proximity threshold matches how choreographers judge collisions or safety. No distance threshold is discussed. [I-01]
- Travel distance is a meaningful pain point or decision criterion. The interview discusses smooth and logical transitions but does not mention measuring distance. [I-01]
- Users want automated target-position swaps. No reassignment or optimization request is reported. [I-01]
- Pairwise destination swapping would produce artistically, physically, or role-appropriate results. No evidence addresses dancer-specific constraints. [I-01]
- Users need dancer locks to protect assignments. No fixed-role or non-reassignable dancer constraint is reported. [I-01]
- Users need accept, reject, and undo controls for recommendations. No recommendation workflow was tested or discussed. [I-01]
- Collision detection would have prevented the errors reported. Some errors involved exits or moving through formations incorrectly and may not be proximity problems. [I-01]
- The proposed product would save a meaningful portion of the reported 5–8 hours. The participant attributed the greatest burden to designing formations themselves. [I-01]
- Users will replace or pay for a new product rather than continue with Google Slides, Arrange Us, and rehearsal-based correction. [I-01]
- Local-only individual use is sufficient. The participant valued sharing and independent dancer access, although real-time collaboration was not mentioned. [I-01]
- Two saved formations are sufficient context for the workflow. The participant managed roughly 20–30 formations and communicated changes sequentially across them. [I-01]
- The product's value is primarily safety rather than communication, memory, visual design, or rehearsal efficiency. The interview contains evidence for all of those concerns but does not rank them beyond formation design being most time-consuming. [I-01]

## Recommended MVP Priority

### Priority 0: Validate before full implementation

1. Test whether choreographers can and want to model two adjacent formations in a lightweight prototype. The participant already uses visual planning tools, which supports testing this interaction but does not prove demand for a new product. [I-01]
2. Test straight-line transition playback as the primary value proposition. Visual movement simulation is the clearest directly stated product interest. [I-01]
3. Observe whether playback reveals errors that the choreographer would otherwise miss. I-01 reported collisions despite pre-rehearsal review, but the notes do not establish the cause or the effectiveness of automated analysis. [I-01]
4. Compare the prototype directly with Google Slides and Arrange Us on speed, clarity, and dancer comprehension. Both are established parts of I-01's current workflow. [I-01]

### Build first only if prototype validation is positive

- Create and label dancers. [I-01]
- Place the same dancers in Formation A and Formation B. [I-01]
- Animate the transition between the two formations. [I-01]
- Provide an understandable visual representation that can be reviewed before rehearsal. [I-01]

This narrow slice maps to the observed use of visual formation tools and the explicitly stated value of movement simulation. [I-01]

### Validate next

- Collision or proximity highlighting, because I-01 reported collisions but did not define safe distance or confirm that automated detection would solve the reported errors. [I-01]
- A way for dancers to reference positions outside rehearsal, because Google Slides' independent accessibility was explicitly useful. The correct product response—export, share link, or no additional feature—remains unvalidated. [I-01]
- Support for navigating a sequence longer than two formations, because I-01 described performances with 20–30 formations and sequential communication. This may conflict with a two-formation-only product model. [I-01]

### Defer

- Travel-distance measurement [I-01]
- Automated target-position swaps [I-01]
- Dancer locks [I-01]
- Recommendation acceptance, rejection, and undo [I-01]
- Any safety claim [I-01]

None of these capabilities was directly requested, used, or evaluated in I-01. [I-01]

## Evidence Against Building the Product

- **Only one interview exists.** There is no evidence of repeated demand or repeated pain across the target market. [I-01]
- **The participant already uses a direct competitor or adjacent product.** Arrange Us is part of the workflow, but the notes identify no dissatisfaction, missing feature, or reason to switch. [I-01]
- **Google Slides provides valued functionality.** It supports design, sharing, and independent dancer reference, creating a familiar low-cost substitute. [I-01]
- **The proposed core job may not address the largest burden.** The participant said designing formations themselves was the most time-consuming step, while FormationFlow is scoped around transitions between already-defined formations. [I-01]
- **The observed errors may be experience-dependent.** The participant was designing formations for the first time, so collision severity may decline with experience rather than remain a durable market problem. [I-01]
- **Existing human workarounds appear functional.** Pre-review reduced errors; rehearsal collaboration clarified paths; repeated practice reinforced revisions. The notes do not show that these methods failed to deliver an acceptable outcome. [I-01]
- **Some reported errors fall outside the proposed model.** Incorrect exits and movement through formations may require timing, pathways, entrances, exits, or instruction—not only straight-line proximity analysis. [I-01]
- **The real workflow spans many formations.** A performance contained roughly 20–30 formations, while a product limited to two formations at a time may add setup and navigation overhead. [I-01]
- **No commercial evidence is present.** There is no willingness-to-pay signal, adoption commitment, switching intent, retention evidence, or quantified cost of the current process. [I-01]
- **No evidence supports the differentiating optimization features.** Travel metrics, swaps, locks, and recommendation controls are absent from the interview. [I-01]

## Final Recommendation

### MODIFY

Do not proceed with the full FormationFlow MVP as currently scoped. Proceed with a narrower validation phase focused on two-formation setup and visual transition playback. This preserves the one product benefit explicitly supported by the interview while avoiding investment in unsupported optimization features. [I-01]

Advance from prototype to MVP only if additional research establishes all of the following:

1. Transition errors recur among multiple choreographers, including people with prior formation-design experience. [I-01]
2. Visual playback exposes meaningful errors earlier than Google Slides, Arrange Us, or existing rehearsal practices. [I-01]
3. Straight-line two-formation modeling represents a common real workflow. [I-01]
4. Automated proximity detection matches users' judgment of unsafe movement. [I-01]
5. At least one proposed differentiator—distance measurement, target swapping, or locks—changes a real choreography decision. [I-01]
6. The resulting time or rehearsal savings are large enough to motivate adoption or switching. [I-01]

None of these six conditions is established by I-01 alone. [I-01]
